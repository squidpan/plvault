# Story 5 — Building Runbooks and Operational Knowledge

## Why This Story?

This demonstrates:

- documentation
    
- operational excellence
    
- customer support
    
- repeatability
    
- continuous improvement
    
- implementation maturity
    

Many candidates underestimate how important this is.

Professional Services organizations don't just deliver software.

They deliver **supportable solutions**.

---

# Situation

Throughout my career, particularly on the Federal Reserve project, I realized that many operational procedures existed only in people's heads.

That worked while experienced team members were available.

But it created risk whenever:

- new people joined
    
- production issues occurred
    
- deployments happened after hours
    
- support responsibilities changed
    

---

# Challenge

The challenge wasn't simply writing documentation.

It was capturing operational knowledge in a way that someone else could successfully execute it.

Good documentation should answer:

- What changed?
    
- Why did it change?
    
- How do I verify it's working?
    
- What should I check first if something fails?
    
- When should I escalate?
    
- Who owns each step?
    

---

# My Role

I helped create and maintain documentation including:

- deployment runbooks
    
- production validation checklists
    
- troubleshooting procedures
    
- operational support documentation
    
- release notes
    
- implementation guidance
    

I viewed documentation as part of the implementation—not something done afterward if there was time.

---

# Example

Before a production deployment, a runbook might include:

### Before Deployment

- Verify deployment package
    
- Confirm approvals
    
- Check environment readiness
    
- Validate dependencies
    
- Notify stakeholders
    

### During Deployment

- Execute deployment steps
    
- Record progress
    
- Monitor logs
    
- Verify expected behavior
    

### After Deployment

- Execute smoke tests
    
- Validate business functions
    
- Confirm integrations
    
- Check dashboards
    
- Obtain business sign-off
    

That documentation helped make deployments more predictable and repeatable.

---

# Result

Having consistent runbooks and support documentation provided several benefits:

- faster onboarding of new team members
    
- more consistent production support
    
- fewer missed deployment steps
    
- improved communication across teams
    
- better knowledge retention
    

Most importantly, operational knowledge became a team asset rather than depending on individual memory.

---

# Why This Relates to SPG

One reason this resonates with me is that customer implementations don't end when the software is configured.

A successful implementation also includes:

- administrator documentation
    
- support procedures
    
- troubleshooting guidance
    
- testing evidence
    
- deployment documentation
    
- operational handoff
    

Those artifacts help the customer succeed long after the consultants have finished the implementation.

---

# What I Learned

One lesson I've learned is:

> **A successful implementation is one that another person can operate confidently after you've left.**

That's the real test.

# The Five Stories as a Complete Set

Now that we've built all five, notice how they mirror the lifecycle of an enterprise implementation:

|Story|Phase of the Lifecycle|What It Demonstrates|
|---|---|---|
|**Story 1**|Discovery → Solution Design|Understanding the business, modernization, translating requirements, cross-functional collaboration|
|**Story 2**|Configuration → Testing|Preventing defects, validating business rules, improving quality before QA|
|**Story 3**|Deployment → Go-Live|Release readiness, production planning, operational coordination|
|**Story 4**|Production Support|Structured troubleshooting, evidence-based investigation, customer communication|
|**Story 5**|Operational Handoff → Continuous Improvement|Documentation, knowledge transfer, supportability, operational excellence|

I don't think that's a coincidence—that sequence is very close to the lifecycle we've been studying for Surefyre.

## One recommendation before we move on

I would make one more addition to your interview preparation that I think will have a huge payoff.

Let's create a **story index**.

Instead of memorizing all five stories, build a table like this:

|If they ask...|Use Story|
|---|---|
|Tell me about yourself|Story 0|
|Biggest project|Story 1|
|Complex implementation|Story 1|
|APIs / modernization|Story 1|
|Testing|Story 2|
|Quality improvement|Story 2|
|Deployment|Story 3|
|Go-live|Story 3|
|Troubleshooting|Story 4|
|Production support|Story 4|
|Documentation|Story 5|
|Knowledge transfer|Story 5|
|Customer readiness|Story 5|

That way you're not trying to remember five separate speeches. You're remembering **one implementation lifecycle** and selecting the story that best fits the interviewer's question. I think that's a much more natural and effective way to prepare.
