## The most popular study strategy is ineffective, but nearly everyone uses it

When I was still in my professor job, I used to teach a few classes on learning and memory to first-year psychology undergrads.

Every year, I asked them the same question: “Who relies on reading and rereading as their main study strategy?”

And every year, most hands went up.

It makes sense because reading and rereading feels like making progress. And maybe it worked well for them before entering the university. But here’s the truth: Rereading has [little added benefit](https://sites.wustl.edu/mdl1/files/2026/06/Karpicke-et-al.-2009-Metacognitive-strategies-in-student-learning-Do-students-practise-retrieval-when-they-study-on-thei.pdf) to initially reading the material, yet it’s the strategy students use the most:

![A table shoing study strategies in a student sample, ranked by popularity. 83.6% use rereading, and for 54.8%, rereading is their №1 study strategy.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*B9P1_hMeT-pN_pThbmjrHQ.png)

There are science-backed strategies that work a lot better than rereading.

Here are my 7 top strategies to maximize learning (==and #4 explains why rereading isn’t one of them)==:

### 1\. Elaborate on what you learn.

Connect new information to what you already know and [relate it to yourself](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1008&context=chip_docs).

==If you can form connections between pieces of knowledge, they’re no longer isolated but rather part of the bigger picture, and it will be easier to access them. It is especially helpful if you can relate the information to your story and experiences.==

By elaborating on information this way, you also engage more actively with the new knowledge, which supports learning.

Suggestions:

- Try relating the new content to something in your life or about yourself.
- Find relations to other things you already know.
- Try creating a mental image of the content.

### 2\. Test yourself often.

Testing is one of the most [effective techniques for learning](https://www.academia.edu/download/45684358/McDaniel_JEP.pdf). It should be a regular learning strategy, not just something you do in an exam. Yet, students [rarely use this strategy](https://sites.wustl.edu/mdl1/files/2026/06/Karpicke-et-al.-2009-Metacognitive-strategies-in-student-learning-Do-students-practise-retrieval-when-they-study-on-thei.pdf).

Testing works because instead of just “putting in” new information, you practice retrieving it, as in an exam. It’s also the best strategy to reveal what you do not yet know, so you’re more aware of what to focus on.

Suggestions:

- Use flashcards as a study tool, with the key concepts/questions on one side and the solutions on the other.
- Use textbook or online practice questions (or generate your own or use AI) to test yourself. Try answering them without looking.
- Try to explain what you’re learning to yourself or another person.

### 3\. Organize the information.

Build structure: topics, subtopics, and relationships. Organized knowledge is easier to retrieve because your brain has a map. When information is grouped into topics, subtopics, and relationships, one idea can help you access another.

Grouping information into larger units also reduces mental load.

Suggestions:

- ==Create mind maps, tables, or diagrams.==
- Organize information by topics and subtopics.

### 4\. Watch out for illusions of competence.

I started this article with my students’ favorite study strategy: Reading and rereading. Students frequently use the strategy of rereading textbook content or their notes, which could create [illusions of competence](https://sites.wustl.edu/mdl1/files/2026/06/Karpicke-et-al.-2009-Metacognitive-strategies-in-student-learning-Do-students-practise-retrieval-when-they-study-on-thei.pdf): The belief that you know it when you actually don’t.

If you reread the information, it will feel easy and fluent, which is why people love this strategy. But: It feels easy because you’ve seen it before, not because you know it. Recognizing it as familiar is different from being able to retrieve it on your own.

Suggestions:

- ==Whenever you find yourself rereading a text or your notes, put them away and try to use only your brain to see if you really know it.==
- ==Try some of the other strategies from this article to learn more effectively.==

### 5\. Consider your inner state and outer context as you learn.

The whole learning situation matters. Your brain stores not only the content you’re studying, but also characteristics of the learning situation: your surroundings and how you feel.

There’s a super cool [study from 1975](https://columbia-sipps.github.io/workshop_files/2023/prof-dev/Godden1975_ContextDependentMemory.pdf) in which divers learned a word list either on land or underwater and later were tested on land or underwater. Results showed that those who changed the context (i.e., studied underwater but were tested on land or vice versa) remembered fewer words than those who studied and retrieved the list in the same context.

Here’s the graphical version of the study:

![Graphical depiction of the results from the Godden and Baddeley (1975) experiment.](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/format:webp/1*9FrLtMCEpcOxUR3Z_ENC5g.jpeg)

Results from the Godden and Baddeley (1975) experiment. Source

Another [key study](http://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Eich%20Metcalfe%201989.pdf) had participants study either in a happy or in a sad mood. For the subsequent memory test, participants were again in either a happy or a sad mood. You may imagine the results: Those who were in the same mood during learning and testing performed better than those who changed moods.

These studies suggest that sometimes your inner and outer contexts can facilitate access to knowledge. It doesn’t mean you need to simulate the exact exam situation while you study, but if you can create some similarity, it could be an additional support for your memory.

Suggestions:

- Make your learning situation and the situation where you must reproduce the content more similar.
- Anticipate the exam situation while you’re studying: Where will you be and how will you feel?
- Regarding your external study environment, you can also switch between different contexts during your study sessions, so the knowledge becomes less dependent on your surroundings.
- As discussed under #2, also practice testing yourself because that’s what you’ll be doing in an exam, and it additionally makes your study conditions more similar to exam conditions.

### 6\. Space out your learning sessions.

What’s better, a continuous 8-hour learning session or 4 sessions of 2 hours each?

According to science, it’s the second one: spacing out learning sessions [helps you remember the content better](https://escholarship.org/content/qt1n15d7xr/qt1n15d7xr.pdf). It’s called the spacing effect.

Suggestions:

- Instead of one long study session, divide your studies into several shorter sessions, ideally over different days.
- If you divide them over several days, you additionally reap the benefits of sleep on learning, as covered in the next point.

### 7\. Leverage sleep.

Sleep plays a major role in memory consolidation. During sleep, new information can be [stabilized, reorganized, and integrated](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3768102/) with what you already know.

Sleep is essential for learning, and sleep loss impairs attention and memory.

Suggestions:

- Make sleep your top priority.
- Don’t sacrifice sleep for more study time (pulling all-nighters is common among students, but not a recommended strategy!).
- Move your study sessions to late afternoons and evenings, or do a recap before bed if you study earlier in the day. What you study before sleep is more likely to be prioritized for overnight consolidation.

Your overall health habits influence your learning and memory, and sleep is one of the fundamentals.

## Wrapping up

Not all learning techniques are effective. In fact, the most popular technique (rereading) feels productive, but it’s ineffective.

Even if you’ve been doing fine so far with rereading, you might want to give these 7 strategies a try.
