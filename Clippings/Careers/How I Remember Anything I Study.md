You don’t remember by trying to remember.

==C== ==**ounterintuitive**==, isn’t it? But here’s the **lesson I learned over the years**: the more effort you put into rereading, highlighting, and perhaps taking some notes, the more likely it is that the information will **not** stick in your head.

Perhaps you experienced this as a student in high school or university.

You have some pages to study for your exam. You sit down and start learning. You highlight and reread them. You become more fluent in reading what you need to study. “ *Great! I finished studying. I’m ready for my exam,*” you think.

The next day, you try remembering this hours before your big exam.

**You realize you can’t**. You need to check your highlights or the correct answers to do so. **Anxiety increases**. **Stress** burns your head. You feel you learned **nothing**. You feel you are not ready. Worse, not smart enough.

Did this happen to you?

Well, this is the cognitive **illusion of learning**.

To truly learn anything you study, you should do this:

## How To Actually Remember What You Study

One word: **retrieval**.

*What is that?* Well, it is a strategy to bring information to mind. By doing this, you help **link information** from your **short-term memory** to your **long-term memory** by recalling what you learned so far.

You (*can*) practice retrieval in many situations:

- When testing yourself.
- When doing elaborative interrogation (asking and answering how and why questions).
- When closing your book and doing free recall.
- When studying using flashcards.

My **secret** to remembering anything I study is to use these techniques.

For example, when reading a book, I always make flashcards to answer questions about the topic. Or sometimes I close it and recall what I just read or explain the topic in my own words. Other times, I decide to write an article about it.

All those behaviors help me link information to my long-term memory.

Importantly, I use it too. For example, when talking and debating with colleagues, or when doing a routine, speaking a language that I’m learning daily, etc. But if you don’t use this information, then the chances that it doesn’t link to your long-term memory are high.

So, to understand why this technique is so effective, it is worth knowing a bit about how memory works.

## The Psychology of Memory

Memory can be divided into:

1. **Short** -term memory: lasts seconds or minutes.
2. **Long** -term memory: lasts hours, days, months, years, or a lifetime.

Now, learning is about linking information to your long-term memory.

When you reread and highlight (or both), you store the information in your short-term memory. This gives you a **false sense of understanding** by gaining fluency in what you read.

Basically, the more you read, the more confident you feel with the topic. **But close the book** and try to recall or explain what you just read, and you will **immediately** see that was an illusion.

This also happens when you learn something by **re-watching** videos.

Retrieval is way more effective than those techniques because it brings information to your mind and links it to your long-term memory. Plus, it improves your metacognition by helping you understand what you know and what you don’t about a topic.

The more effort you put into retrieving information, the better you learn.

## How To Use Retrieval

- Always test yourself.
- Use [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/) to create flashcards.
- Close your notes or summaries and try to recall what you learned so far.
- Create and answer what, how, and why questions about what you are learning.
- Create tests with AI to understand what you know and don’t know.

## 2 Tips To Practice Retrieval

So, if you want to remember: retrieve and test yourself.

It will be **uncomfortable.** No one likes being exposed to what we don’t know (I think this is one of the reasons we test ourselves at the last minute for a big exam: the illusion of learning gives us peace at a low cognitive cost).

But if you *really* want to remember what you study, retrieval is the first technique you must incorporate into your learning routine.

Here are 2 tips to include it in your study sessions.

## Tip 1: free-recall

1. Close your book.
2. Recall what you learned so far (without looking at your book).
3. Get feedback from the right answer.
4. See what you know and don’t know.
5. Repeat.

## Tip 2: flashcards

1. Create flashcards from your material.
2. Answer the questions honestly, without looking at the correct answers.
3. Test yourself and get feedback.
4. See what you know and don’t know.
5. Repeat.
